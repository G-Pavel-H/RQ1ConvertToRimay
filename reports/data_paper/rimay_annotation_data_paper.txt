\begin{abstract}
Incompleteness is one of the most frequently cited requirement defects, yet the
requirements engineering (RE) literature offers no shared operational definition
of it. Existing datasets label a requirement as incomplete when it ``lacks
crucial information'', a criterion that cannot be located in the text and that
annotators apply inconsistently. We present a dataset that grounds
incompleteness in the grammar of Rimay, a controlled natural language for
functional requirements. Fifty feature requests from the issue trackers of two
open-source messaging applications were annotated by three annotators, who
converted each request into Rimay and judged each of its five structural slots
(scope, condition, actor, modal verb, action) as \slotP, \slotI{} or \slotM. A
requirement is incomplete when a slot the grammar requires (actor, modal verb or
action) is missing. Requests that bundle several system responses were
adjudicated as non-atomic and decomposed into atomic units, yielding 77 units in
total (35 atomic requests and 15 non-atomic requests split into 42 units). Every
gold unit was validated with Paska, a Rimay conformance and smell checker. We
describe the annotation scheme, the tool, the training and pilot rounds, the
agreement obtained, and the curation procedure, and we report the difficulties
that converting informal, multi-request user text into a controlled language
creates. Agreement on the incompleteness label is fair by Fleiss' $\kappa$
(.32) but substantial by Gwet's AC1 (.72), a gap explained by the rarity of the
label; agreement on atomicity is moderate ($\kappa=.60$). Under our definition,
4 of the 35 atomic requests are incomplete, against 23 under the original
corpus labels, all four of ours falling inside theirs. The dataset, the
annotation guide, the tool and the curation provenance are released to support
the evaluation of automated incompleteness detection and requirement
conversion.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} requirements engineering, incompleteness,
controlled natural language, Rimay, annotation, dataset, feature requests

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Incomplete requirements are a long-standing cause of rework and
misunderstanding in software projects~\cite{todo-incompleteness-survey}.
Despite this, ``incomplete'' remains loosely defined. Studies either leave the
judgement to the annotator (``the requirement lacks information needed to
describe the intended behaviour'') or tie it to a specific artefact, such as a
missing use-case step or an uncovered input. Neither gives a criterion that is
both general and locatable: an annotator who calls a requirement incomplete
cannot point to what is missing, and two annotators who disagree cannot tell
whether they disagree about the requirement or about the definition.

Large language models (LLMs) make this gap pressing. They are increasingly
proposed as assistants that rewrite, structure or check requirements, and
evaluating them requires a reference that says, for each requirement, whether
it is incomplete and \emph{why}. A binary label cannot distinguish a model that
correctly infers an obvious actor from one that invents a missing action.

We address this by grounding incompleteness in a controlled natural language
(CNL). Rimay~\cite{veizaga2021rimay} prescribes a fixed structure for
functional requirements: an optional scope, an optional condition, and a
mandatory system response formed by an actor, a modal verb and an action
phrase. Converting a requirement into Rimay forces the annotator to fill each
slot, and the slots that cannot be filled are exactly the information the
requirement does not give. This yields an operational definition:
\begin{quote}
\emph{A requirement is incomplete when a slot that the Rimay grammar requires
(actor, modal verb or action) is missing from it, i.e.\ is neither stated nor
reasonably inferable from the text.}
\end{quote}

This paper describes a dataset built on this definition. Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item An annotation scheme that records, for every requirement, a Rimay
        conversion and a three-valued judgement (\slotP, \slotI, \slotM) for
        each of the five Rimay slots, from which incompleteness is derived
        rather than asserted (Section~\ref{sec:scheme}).
  \item A curated gold standard of 50 feature requests (77 atomic units), with
        the original per-annotator annotations, the adjudicated labels, the
        Rimay conversions, Paska validation results and per-unit provenance
        (Sections~\ref{sec:curation} and~\ref{sec:dataset}).
  \item An account of the annotation process, including a pilot, agreement
        statistics that are robust to label prevalence, and the practical
        difficulties of converting informal user text into a CNL, notably
        implicit slots and non-atomic requests
        (Sections~\ref{sec:process}--\ref{sec:challenges}).
  \item A comparison with the incompleteness labels of the source corpus, which
        shows how much an unbounded definition inflates the label
        (Section~\ref{sec:pragyan}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Rimay}
Rimay is a CNL for functional requirements, derived from an analysis of
industrial requirements in the financial domain~\cite{veizaga2021rimay}. A
Rimay requirement has up to three segments in a fixed order:
\begin{center}
\rim{[Scope] [Condition] Actor ModalVerb ActionPhrase}
\end{center}
The scope (e.g.\ ``For all the depositories,'') restricts what the requirement
applies to; the condition gates the system response and is a precondition
(``If \ldots''), a trigger (``When \ldots'') or a temporal clause (``Before
\ldots'', ``Every day \ldots''); the actor, the modal verb (\emph{must},
\emph{shall}, \emph{will}) and the action phrase form the system response.
Scope and condition are optional; the system response is mandatory. Rimay
requires each requirement to be \emph{atomic}: it expresses one system
response. The combinations of segments define ten patterns.

\subsection{Paska}
Paska~\cite{veizaga2024paska} parses requirements against the Rimay grammar and
reports \emph{smells}, i.e.\ quality issues such as a missing system response,
a condition placed after the response, a non-atomic action or an imprecise
verb. We use it as an automated conformance check on every gold conversion
(Section~\ref{sec:paska}).

\subsection{Source corpus}
The requirements come from the corpus of Pragyan et
al.~\cite{pragyan2025}: 100 feature requests collected from the public issue
trackers of the Signal and Mastodon messaging applications. Each request was
labelled by their annotators for incompleteness, defined as requests that
``lack crucial information needed to fully describe the intended system
behavior'', together with free-text notes and clarification questions. The
labels carry no span and no sub-type. Their reported agreement on
incompleteness varies across annotation batches ($\kappa$ = .65, .40 and .87).
% TODO: check the exact wording and kappa values against the published paper

\subsection{Agreement under skewed labels}
Cohen's and Fleiss' $\kappa$~\cite{fleiss1971} correct observed agreement for
chance using the marginal label distribution. When one category dominates,
expected agreement approaches 1 and $\kappa$ can be low or negative even when
annotators agree on almost every item, the so-called \emph{kappa
paradox}~\cite{feinstein1990}. Gwet's AC1~\cite{gwet2008} uses a chance model
that is far less sensitive to prevalence. Because several of our labels are
heavily skewed (e.g.\ incompleteness is rare; the modal verb is almost always
implied), we report raw agreement, the proportion of unanimous items, Fleiss'
$\kappa$ and Gwet's AC1 together, and read them jointly. $\kappa$ values are
interpreted with the bands of Landis and Koch~\cite{landis1977}.

\section{Annotation scheme}\label{sec:scheme}

For each feature request an annotator performs one task with two outputs: a
Rimay conversion of the request and a judgement on each structural slot. The
full guide (about 2{,}000 words, with worked examples) is embedded in the
annotation tool and released with the dataset.

\subsection{Slot judgements}
Each of the five slots is judged as:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[\slotP] the information is explicitly stated;
  \item[\slotI] it is not stated, but a reasonable reader infers it from
        context (e.g.\ in ``I'd like a preview in the notification bar'' the
        actor is obviously the application);
  \item[\slotM] it is not stated and cannot reasonably be inferred; a developer
        would need to ask a clarifying question to fill it.
\end{description}
The distinction between \slotI{} and \slotM{} is the central judgement of the
scheme. A system that fills an implied slot has understood the context; a
system that fills a missing slot has invented information.

\subsection{Derived incompleteness}
Annotators do not label incompleteness directly. The tool computes it: a
requirement is incomplete if and only if at least one mandatory slot (actor,
modal verb, action) is \slotM. A missing scope or condition does not make a
requirement incomplete. Deriving the verdict from the slots means every
incompleteness label points to the slot that causes it.

\subsection{Further fields}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Condition type} (precondition, trigger, temporal, none), when a
        condition is present or implied.
  \item \textbf{Non-atomic flag} and the number of system responses, when the
        request asks for two or more distinct actions. Several objects of a
        single action (``send a confirmation and a settlement instruction'')
        do not make a request non-atomic.
  \item \textbf{Rimay conversion}, following the guide's rules: segment order;
        \emph{must} as the default modal verb for wishes (``it would be nice
        if \ldots''), with the modal slot marked \slotI; concrete names and
        values in double quotes; generic actors (\emph{the App}); and a
        placeholder for every missing slot (\rim{<MISSING\_SCOPE>},
        \rim{<MISSING\_CONDITION>}, \rim{<MISSING\_ACTOR>},
        \rim{<MISSING\_MODAL\_VERB>}, \rim{<MISSING\_ACTION>}), so that the
        text and the slot grid agree. Implied slots are written out, not
        replaced by a placeholder.
  \item \textbf{Notes}, free text for judgement calls.
\end{itemize}
An earlier version of the form also asked for the Rimay pattern number. We
removed it before the pilot: the pattern is fully determined by the slot and
condition-type choices, so recording it separately would only add spurious
disagreement.

\section{Data and annotation process}\label{sec:process}

\subsection{Sampling}
From the 100 requests of the source corpus we drew three disjoint sets: 4 for
training, 10 for the pilot and 50 for the main study (64 in total). The main
set contains 47 Signal and 3 Mastodon requests; 29 of the 50 are labelled
incomplete in the source corpus.
% TODO: state how the 64 were selected (random? stratified by Pragyan label?)

\subsection{Annotators}
The training and pilot rounds were annotated by four people: the first author
and three annotators with a background in software engineering, here A1, A2 and
A3. The main set was annotated by A1, A2 and A3; the first author acted as
administrator and adjudicator and did not contribute main-set annotations.
% TODO: annotator background (degree level, RE experience), compensation,
% and whether names may be released (currently pseudonymised as A1-A3)

\subsection{Tool}
Annotation was carried out in a purpose-built web application (Angular front
end, Node.js/Express back end, MongoDB database), released with the dataset.
Its main properties are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Blind annotation.} Each annotator sees only their own work and
        never sees the source-corpus labels or the other annotators' answers.
  \item \textbf{Guided input.} The form presents the slot grid next to the
        conversion box, offers one-click insertion of the missing-slot
        placeholders, and displays the derived complete/incomplete verdict as
        the annotator works.
  \item \textbf{Phases.} Requirements are grouped by phase (training, pilot,
        main) so that agreement and export are computed per phase.
  \item \textbf{Built-in agreement.} An administrator view computes Fleiss'
        and pairwise Cohen's $\kappa$, raw agreement and a disagreement
        worksheet for any phase, and a majority-vote gold for adjudication.
  \item \textbf{Export} of all annotations as CSV and JSON.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training}
All four annotators annotated the four training requests and discussed every
disagreement in a joint session, using the guide as the reference.

\subsection{Pilot}\label{sec:pilot}
The pilot (10 requests, four annotators, 40 annotations) was annotated
independently. Table~\ref{tab:pilot} reports agreement. Raw agreement was high:
on every slot at least three of the four annotators agreed on 90--100\% of the
requests. $\kappa$ was nonetheless low or negative for scope, modal verb and
action, the three slots dominated by a single value, which is the kappa
paradox at work. Incompleteness and atomicity showed no variation at all (every
annotation was complete and atomic), so $\kappa$ was undefined. Condition and
condition type were the genuinely contested fields. Mean pairwise chrF between
conversions was .56; the two most divergent requests were those containing
several actions, where annotators differed on whether to write one or two
system responses.

an unstated scope as \slotI{} (``for all users''); this convention was later
revised during curation (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

\section{Inter-annotator agreement}\label{sec:iaa}

We report agreement on the \emph{original} main-set annotations, before any
curation. Slot and incompleteness agreement is computed on the 30 requests that
no annotator flagged as non-atomic, because a non-atomic request has no single
set of slots to compare. Atomicity agreement is computed on all 50.

\subsection{Slots and incompleteness}
Table~\ref{tab:iaa} gives the results. Raw agreement on incompleteness is
80\%, with 70\% of requests unanimous, yet Fleiss' $\kappa$ is only .32
(fair). Only 16 of the 90 judgements (18\%) are \emph{incomplete}, and at this
prevalence each disagreement weighs heavily on $\kappa$. Gwet's AC1, which is
robust to prevalence, is .72. The pairwise picture is uneven: A1 and A3 reach
$\kappa=.61$ (substantial), while A2 flags more requests (7, against 5 and 4)
and agrees less with both ($\kappa$ = .17 and .23). The disagreements
concentrate in the action slot, i.e.\ in deciding whether a vague wish states
an action at all.

The slot results show the same pattern. Scope and modal verb, where almost
every judgement takes one value, have near-zero $\kappa$ but AC1 near .8.
Condition is the most reliably annotated slot by both measures. Actor is the
weakest by both, and its errors are mostly \slotP{} versus \slotI: whether a
request that mentions ``Signal'' or ``the app'' in passing states the actor or
leaves it implied. This distinction does not affect incompleteness.

\subsection{Atomicity}
Twenty of the 50 requests were flagged as non-atomic by at least one annotator
(8 by all three, 6 by two, 6 by one); the annotators flagged 13, 10 and 19.
Agreement is moderate: raw agreement 84\%, 76\% unanimous, Fleiss' $\kappa$
.60, AC1 .73, pairwise $\kappa$ between .58 and .64. Annotators did not all
record the flag the same way: one used the dedicated checkbox, another wrote
the placeholder \rim{<NON\_ATOMIC>} in the conversion and explained it in the
notes without ticking the box. We therefore count a request as flagged when
any of the three signals (checkbox, placeholder, note) is present; reading the
checkbox alone would have reported this annotator as never flagging a
non-atomic request.

\subsection{Conversions}
Conversion agreement was measured on the 30 atomic requests as the pairwise
cosine similarity of sentence embeddings (all-MiniLM~\cite{todo-minilm}) and as
a character-level sequence ratio. Median cosine similarity is .78 (mean .66)
and the mean sequence ratio is .58. The low means are driven by a few pairs in
which one annotator gave an empty or placeholder-only conversion.

\section{Adjudication and curation}\label{sec:curation}

The original annotations are released unchanged. Curation produced a separate
gold layer in four steps, carried out by the first author with a dedicated
local curation tool (released with the dataset) that records the original
value, the curated value, the reason for each change and its provenance.
% TODO: state whether disputed cases were discussed with A1-A3

\subsection{Atomicity adjudication}
Each of the 20 flagged requests was adjudicated with a single rule:
\emph{distinct system responses are separate units; alternatives, examples,
rationale, details of one action, and a condition together with its response
are not}. For example, a request offering three alternative implementations of
one behaviour (``Idea 1 / 2 / 3'') remains one unit. By this rule 15 requests
are non-atomic and 5 are atomic, giving 35 atomic and 15 non-atomic requests.

\subsection{Decomposition into units}
Each non-atomic request was decomposed into atomic units, each with its own
Rimay conversion, slot judgements and condition type. The 15 requests yield 42
units (nine split into 2, three into 3, and one each into 4, 5 and 6). For a
small number of requests, annotators had written per-response conversions,
which were split into units directly. For most, annotators had marked the
request non-atomic without writing the individual responses, as the guide
instructed them to convert only the primary action. For these, unit texts and
slot labels were produced during curation (Section~\ref{sec:ai}). Because the
units of non-atomic requests were not produced independently by three
annotators, we do not report inter-annotator agreement on them.

\subsection{Slot gold and text refinement}
For every unit and slot, the gold value is the strict majority of the three
annotators; 32 of the 77 units had at least one slot on which annotators
disagreed. Conversions were then refined so that each text agrees with its
slot grid and with the guide. The recorded reasons for changes are: rewriting
an ungrounded scope as a placeholder (82 changes, Section~\ref{sec:scope}),
typographical fixes (41), aligning the text with the annotator's own slot
judgements (26), word choice (17), adding the \emph{then} that Rimay requires
after a condition (11), and correcting a conversion that misstated the request
(3). Slot judgements were never changed to fit a text; where the two
disagreed, the text was changed.

\subsection{Scope convention}\label{sec:scope}
Following the pilot convention, annotators recorded an unstated scope as
\slotI{} in 91\% of their judgements. During curation we reversed this: a
scope that the request does not state and that cannot be grounded in it is
\slotM{} and is written \rim{<MISSING\_SCOPE>}. Writing an implied
``For all users,'' adds content the user never expressed, and it makes every
conversion begin with the same boilerplate, which inflates text similarity.
Because scope is optional, the change does not affect any incompleteness label.
In the gold, 76 of the 77 units have a missing scope.

\subsection{Paska validation}\label{sec:paska}
Paska was run on every curated conversion (231 annotator-unit records). For
213 records the annotator judged the unit complete and Paska found no smell.
For 14 records the annotator judged the unit incomplete and Paska rejected it,
as expected, because the text contains a placeholder. This leaves 4
disagreements (Paska and annotator verdicts agree on 98.3\%): three are the
same conversion of one request, where a trailing ``unless'' clause is read as
a condition placed after the system response, and one is a conversion with an
unquoted conjunction (``keep the archive and blocked flags''), read as two
actions (Section~\ref{sec:challenges}).

Each row carries the request identifier, the source text, the source-corpus

\subsection{Provenance}\label{sec:provenance}
Every unit in \texttt{curation.json} carries a provenance value:
\emph{annotator} (the annotator's text, unchanged), \emph{annotator-refined}
(the annotator's text with curation edits), \emph{annotator-split} (the
annotator's text split into units) or \emph{curation} (no usable annotator
text existed for this unit; written during curation). Among the 105 atomic
records, 9 are unchanged, 90 refined and 6 written during curation. Among the
126 split-unit records, 12 derive from annotator text and 114 were written
during curation. Users who need purely annotator-authored text can filter on
this field; the original annotations are always available unchanged.

\section{Comparison with the source labels}\label{sec:pragyan}

The source corpus labels 23 of our 35 atomic requests as incomplete; our gold
labels 4, and all 4 are among the 23 (agreement 46\%, Cohen's
$\kappa=.13$). On the 15 non-atomic requests, the source labels 6 as
incomplete and our gold none. The requests both label as incomplete contain no
actionable request at all (e.g.\ ``Better identity key synchronization'').
Most of the remaining source-labelled requests are clear, implementable
requests whose clarification questions ask for design decisions (where in the
interface, which default, how an edge case behaves) rather than for missing
requirement content. A label that asks whether a request ``fully describes''
the behaviour can always be pushed further, so it covers most feature requests
and cannot be located in the text. Our slot-level label is deliberately
narrow: it captures the requirement-level gap and leaves design
underspecification to a separate category. We see the two as layers of one
notion of incompleteness rather than as competing definitions.

\section{Challenges and lessons learned}\label{sec:challenges}

\paragraph{Informal text versus a controlled grammar.}
Rimay was designed from formal financial specifications; feature requests are
conversational, emotional and frequently mix the request with rationale,
complaints and examples. The annotators' first task was to locate the
requirement inside the text, and the remaining structure (who acts, under what
condition) is almost always left to the reader. This is why the \slotI{}
value is indispensable: without it, nearly every feature request would be
incomplete, because almost none names the system or uses ``must''.

\paragraph{Implied versus missing.}
The boundary between a slot a reader can infer and a slot a developer would
have to ask about is the hardest judgement in the scheme, and it is where the
remaining incompleteness disagreements lie, almost all in the action slot.
Typical cases are wishes that name a goal but no system behaviour (``the
notifications are annoying'').

\paragraph{Non-atomic requests.}
Thirty percent of the main-set requests bundle several responses. Rimay cannot
express them as one requirement, and deciding what counts as a separate
response is itself contested (alternatives, examples and sub-steps of one
action are not). Annotators also signalled non-atomicity inconsistently
(checkbox, placeholder or note), which a checkbox-only analysis would have
missed. In hindsight, the guide's instruction to convert only the primary
action of a non-atomic request deferred the decomposition to curation; a future
round should ask annotators to write every unit.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The optional scope slot is almost never stated in feature requests. Treating it
as implied by default inserts content and boilerplate, so the convention was
changed from \slotI{} to \slotM{} during curation (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

\paragraph{Tool behaviour.}
Paska encodes conventions of its original domain. It treats some verbs,
notably \emph{support}, as imprecise, a verb that is natural in feature
requests (``support dark mode''). It groups quoted spans only when they use
typographic quotation marks, so an unquoted or straight-quoted name containing
``and'' is read as two actions. And it reads a trailing ``unless'' clause as a
misplaced condition. Paska verdicts should therefore be read together with the
smell type, separating structural smells from stylistic ones.

\paragraph{Agreement statistics.}
With rare labels, $\kappa$ alone understates agreement; with three annotators
and a 15--20\% positive rate, a single disagreement moves $\kappa$
considerably. Reporting raw agreement, unanimity and AC1 alongside $\kappa$,
and reporting pairwise values, makes it possible to locate the source of
disagreement (here, one annotator and one slot).

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Threats to validity and limitations}\label{sec:threats}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Size and prevalence.} The gold contains 50 requests and only 4
        incomplete units. Any classifier evaluated on it will have unstable
        recall and F1 on the incomplete class; we recommend reporting hits and
        false alarms as counts.
  \item \textbf{Single domain.} All requests concern two messaging
        applications; slot distributions will differ in other domains.
  \item \textbf{Curation by one person.} Adjudication and decomposition were
        performed by the first author. The original annotations are released so
        that alternative gold standards can be derived.
  \item \textbf{Units of non-atomic requests} were not independently
        annotated; we report no agreement on them, and the provenance field
        identifies them.
  \item \textbf{Assisted curation and LLM evaluation.} Parts of the curated
        text were drafted with an LLM (Section~\ref{sec:ai}). Studies that
        evaluate LLMs against the gold should consider using the
        annotator-authored subset or the original annotations, identified by
        the provenance field.
  \item \textbf{Guide revision.} The guide was revised after the pilot, and
        the scope convention changed during curation; both changes are
        documented here.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
We release a dataset of feature requests converted into Rimay, in which
incompleteness is defined by the grammar's required slots and every label
points to the slot that causes it. The process showed that the definition can
be applied by annotators with moderate-to-substantial agreement once
prevalence is accounted for, that the \slotI{} category is what makes it
workable on informal text, and that atomicity must be resolved before
incompleteness can be judged. Compared with an open-ended definition, ours
flags far fewer requests, all of them inside the open-ended set, and
separates missing requirement content from open design decisions. We intend the
dataset as a reproducible starting point for evaluating automated
incompleteness detection and requirement conversion, including by LLMs.
