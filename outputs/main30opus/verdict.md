# Cross-strategy verdict — main30opus

Model: `claude-opus-5` · generated 2026-09-17T22:41:42+01:00 · runs: cot, fsl, zsl

The three strategies are not interchangeable — they fail in opposite directions, and the tradeoff is visible in every track.

Start with Track 1, which is measurable here: 31 genuinely-missing slots across 30 requirements, so this isn't the empty-gold artifact case. But the support is concentrated almost entirely in one slot. `condition` carries 21 of the 31 missing labels; scope, actor and modalVerb carry 1–2 each. Any per-slot number outside `condition` and `action` (n=5) rests on one or two instances and should not be read as a difference between strategies. The scope f1 numbers in particular — 0.07 for cot, 0.09 for zsl, 0.0 for fsl — are all a single gold instance against 3–26 false positives and mean nothing individually.

On `condition`, where there is real support, cot and zsl are effectively identical (flag rate 0.857 each, 18/21) and fsl is much worse (0.381, 8/21). That is a real gap: fsl silently invented a triggering condition in 13 of 21 requirements where the source stated none. Its micro flag rate of 0.32 versus ~0.71–0.74 for the other two is the single largest difference in the dataset, and it is the exact failure this experiment exists to detect. fsl's 94% fill rate on *implied* slots confirms the mechanism — the exemplars teach it to produce a complete, well-formed Rimay sentence, and it keeps producing one whether or not the source supports it. Its perfect condition precision (1.0, zero false positives) is the same behaviour seen from the other side: it flags missing almost never, so when it does, it's right.

The inverse holds for cot and zsl: high recall, terrible precision (0.31/0.35 micro), driven by 26 and 20 scope false positives respectively. They over-flag — cot declared 19 of 30 outputs incomplete when gold called only 5 incomplete. Paska corroborates this cleanly: cot's pass rate of 0.37 is almost entirely "Not requirement" (19/19 failures), which is what you get when the model emits a refusal or a partial fragment rather than a requirement. Every cot output judged complete passed paska (11/11). So cot isn't producing bad Rimay — it's declining to produce Rimay.

Track 2 is where fsl wins unambiguously and where the human-human ceiling matters. Humans agree at seq_ratio 0.576 / jaccard 0.473 / cosine 0.663. fsl exceeds all three (0.617 / 0.491 / 0.727) — it is at or above the ceiling, i.e. as close to the humans as the humans are to each other. cot (0.461 / 0.276 / 0.604) and zsl (0.449 / 0.297 / 0.619) sit well below and are indistinguishable from one another; at n=89 pairs with std ~0.24–0.30, the cot–zsl gap is noise, while the fsl–cot gap on jaccard (0.49 vs 0.28) is not.

`action` deserves attention independent of strategy: 5 gold-missing, and all three flag at most 1 (fsl 0/5). Whatever cue signals an absent system response, none of the prompts carry it. That is a prompt-design problem, not a strategy comparison.

What to run next: the honest summary is that fsl produces human-quality Rimay while hallucinating conditions, and cot/zsl refuse rather than hallucinate. Neither is acceptable alone. Test fsl with exemplars that explicitly include a missing-slot case flagged as missing — currently every exemplar presumably shows a complete requirement, which is why it never flags. Separately, n=30 with 21 of 31 missing labels in one slot cannot support per-slot conclusions; expand the gold set with deliberate oversampling of missing actor, modalVerb and action before any of those numbers are reported. And since this is the atomic-only subset, none of this speaks to unfiltered input.
